# OpenVTC Ecosystem Overview

## The Problem

**OpenVTC exists to solve the Know Your Developer problem: how do you know that a contributor to an open-source project is who they claim to be? That their commit history is genuine? That they're not a sock puppet or a compromised account? And increasingly, that they're even human?**

AI agents can now convincingly imitate real developers, committing, reviewing, and communicating in ways indistinguishable from a person. Traditional trust signals like GitHub reputation or commit history are unreliable when they can be generated by a machine. The XZ Utils compromise showed the other failure: an attacker who patiently builds a legitimate-looking history is not caught by any key check at all.

Today, most digital identity is delegated — you prove who you are by showing that some authority (Google, your employer, a government) vouches for you. This works, but it creates dependencies on centralized gatekeepers, locks your identity to their platforms, and breaks down in contexts where no single authority is trusted by all parties.

The OpenVTC ecosystem takes a different approach: **first-person identity**. Instead of asking "who does an authority say you are?", it asks "who do the people around you say you are?" Trust isn't granted by a central authority — it's built from the ground up through real relationships between real people, expressed as cryptographically verifiable credentials. The question becomes not just "who are you?" but "can I verify you're a real person through a chain of real human relationships?" The community this was modeled on from the start is the Linux kernel's maintainers and their aging PGP web of trust; the project was presented to them at the Linux Plumbers Conference in Prague on 2026-10-07 — see [[kernel-web-of-trust]].

## The Big Picture

The ecosystem is a stack of open-source projects that together enable **Verifiable Trust Communities (VTCs)** — groups of people and organizations who establish, verify, and audit trust relationships without relying on a central authority. The system is built on W3C open standards for [[decentralized-identifiers|Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)]] and [[verifiable-credentials|Verifiable Credentials (VCs)]]. [[zero-knowledge-proofs|Zero-knowledge proofs]] let participants verify claims — like humanness, membership, or "enough members vouched for me" — without exposing the underlying data, and the spec recommends ZKP presentation by default.

Here's how the pieces fit together, from bottom to top:

### Layer 1: Identity Infrastructure

At the foundation, every participant needs a cryptographically secure identity — a [[decentralized-identifiers|DID]]. The ecosystem uses [[did-webvh|did:webvh]], a DID method that gives you a portable, self-certifying identifier with a verifiable history of changes. You host your DID document on your own domain or a domain controlled by the provider of your choice, and anyone can verify its entire history of updates, key rotations, and witness attestations.

The [[didwebvh-rs]] library provides the Rust implementation of this DID method (0.8.3 at Fig), and the [[affinidi-webvh-service|did-hosting-service]] runs the infrastructure for hosting and resolving these DIDs at scale, every operation a signed Trust Task. Since Fig, did:webvh is no longer the only identity on offer. **did:webs** — a web-hosted DID backed by a KERI key event log, on KERI v2 through the affinidi-keri library, which joined the coordinated release at Fig — is an identity a VTA can hold from first boot, a VTC can take as its DID and a member can rotate onto, and did-hosting can host one that registers itself. **did:scid** — a did:webvh's self-certifying identifier on its own, without the host in the name, so it never changes when its log moves — arrived alongside as a native identity for personas, signers and administrators. did:webs is still labeled alpha. See [[decentralized-identifiers]] and [[did-types]]. Because a DID is unreadable to humans, the ecosystem added **agent names** in July 2026 — `example.com/@alice` — a shortcut that redirects to a DID and is only ever shown once the DID document claims the name back in `alsoKnownAs`. A person may hold several identifiers with different reach; since Working Draft 02 of the spec the holder *declares* each one's [[correlation-scope]] — pairwise, directed or public — rather than the identifier carrying a fixed type, and the declaration has a name in the credential: `issuerScope`. Since Working Draft 0.7.0 a community, too, may choose a *directed* identifier rather than a public one, bound to the disclosure practice it publishes.

### Layer 2: Key Management and Signing

Your DID is backed by cryptographic keys, but managing keys securely is hard. The [[verifiable-trust-agent|Verifiable Trust Agent (VTA)]] solves this — it's an always-on service that manages your keys, signs things on your behalf, and can run inside hardware-isolated enclaves so your key material never touches an unprotected environment.

The VTA is the heart of the [[verifiable-trust-infrastructure|Verifiable Trust Infrastructure (VTI)]]. It acts as a **signing oracle**: applications ask it to sign credentials, DID updates, messages — and, since Eucalyptus, git commits, so a developer's commit key never leaves the agent. Keys derive from a single seed phrase using [[bip32-key-derivation|BIP-32 derivation]], so backing up one mnemonic protects everything (with non-extractable internal keys as the deliberate exception) — including [[post-quantum-cryptography|post-quantum ML-DSA keys]] alongside Ed25519. It is also the holder's **credential vault** — W3C Data Integrity, BBS, SD-JWT and ISO mdoc — and the holder's *system of record*: a persona store whose **faces** (profiles over an attribute pool, with slots, retirement and composition) sit behind a context boundary, an app-state store, agent memory, and the key custody for [[data-rooms|data rooms]] all live in the VTA. Every door into it is audited in a hash-chained log under its own key, and since Fig a step-up approval actually verifies the user rather than trusting the approving device's word for it.

For most people the VTA runs *in the cloud* — self-hosted, or managed on the **VTA Farm** — while thin *Personal Network Manager* surfaces approve and act on its behalf: the `pnm` CLI, the [[vta-browser-plugin|VTA Wallet browser plugin]], the mobile Authenticator, and now the [[keyring-wallet|Keyring phone wallet]] from Harvard's Berkman Klein Center, which can hold several agents at once and which the Farm can connect to a hosted agent from a single QR code; see [[vta-topology]] for the vocabulary. The Farm itself is open source as three repos: the [[vtafarm|portal]], the [[vtafarm-api|Go/Kubernetes provisioning backend]] and the [[vtafarm-k8s|OpenTofu cluster build]]. Its security model is the point: one Kubernetes namespace per user, every master seed sealed in HashiCorp Vault under a role only that namespace can assume, an operator whose own Vault policy cannot read a seed, and an admin key that is generated on the user's machine and imported — so the Farm hosts your agent but never controls it.

### Layer 3: Secure Communication

Participants communicate over end-to-end encrypted, DID-bound messaging routed through mediators, so participants don't need to know each other's IP addresses. The [[affinidi-tdk|Affinidi Trust Development Kit]] provides the messaging infrastructure; the mediator that routes it — an *operable* one, with queue inspection, a live traffic monitor and runtime configuration, administered only through Trust Tasks — left the TDK for its own repository in October 2026 and is now released on its own cadence. The ecosystem's transport is the lean ToIP [[trust-spanning-protocol|Trust Spanning Protocol (TSP)]], which reached **TSP 1.0** at Fig in all four SDKs — Rust, JavaScript, Go and Dart — every one of which now refuses the earlier drafts. It is on by default in the VTA and the mediator, with relationships formed before any exchange, nested routing across mediators for metadata privacy, and a cross-implementation conformance suite. [[didcomm|DIDComm v2]] remains as the interop carrier on the same mediator, and for the wider Aries/Credo world — including Keyring — the mediator also speaks DIDComm v1.

Every protocol exchange is expressed as a **[[trust-tasks|Trust Task]]** — a versioned JSON document with a canonical `trusttasks.org/spec/*` URI — so the same operation works over TSP, DIDComm, or plain HTTPS. Dogwood made those documents validated, signed by an identified client and replay-guarded at the dispatch spine; **Eucalyptus closed the door**: every call into the stack is a signed Trust Task with a proof bound to its sender, and the remaining REST management surfaces in the VTA, the mediator, DID hosting and the trust registry were removed. The ToIP spec registry the tasks live in — 45 families by Fig, from vetting and rooms to boards, elections, badges and networks — is part of the coordinated release, and since Fig every repository builds against exactly one copy of its crates. Underneath, a reliable **delivery layer** (durable outbox, delivery evidence, ack-only-after-handoff, stored-mail pickup on connect) closes a class of silently lost messages.

### Layer 4: Trust Credentials

Trust relationships are expressed as [[dtg-credentials-overview|Decentralized Trust Graph (DTG) Credentials]] — Verifiable Credential types defined by the Trust Over IP Foundation in what is now titled the [[dtg-credential-spec|DTG Credentials Core Specification]], a formal ToIP deliverable whose **Working Draft 02** (September 2026) reshaped the family around what implementers had learned, whose late-September revisions pinned a **v1 JSON-LD context** and a registry of predicates at `registry.trustoverip.org/dtg`, and whose **Working Draft 0.7.0** (November 2026) salts every credential so digest references cannot be enumerated, lets an authority credential state the purposes its holder may use what it obtains for, and lets networks contain networks. The foundational credentials are:

- **[[membership-credential|Membership Credentials]]** — proof that you are a real, unique *human* within a community (the ecosystem's answer to AI agent impersonation). Membership is a bi-directional pair: a grant from the community and an acknowledgement from the member; since Eucalyptus a membership can also be renewed.
- **[[relationship-credential|Relationship Credentials]]** — peer-to-peer trust attestations between two people. A relationship is verified through a bi-directional pair of VRCs (one each direction forms a complete edge).
- **[[delegation-credential|Delegation]]** and **[[authority-credential|Authority Credentials]]** — a delegation is a third kind of edge (a grant plus a required acceptance) that moves the permission question without answering it; an authority credential is a statement *to a verifier* of what a holder may do, attenuable by the holder down a digest-linked chain. Since DTG Credentials v1, a community's **roles** — vetter, maintainer, custom roles — are authority credentials, and neither VAC nor VDC is a bearer credential any longer.
- **[[statement-credential|Statement Credentials]]** — one type with predicate profiles: the spec defines `endorses/1` ([[endorsement-credential|endorsement]]) and `witnessed/1` ([[witness-credential|witness]], bound to the specific edge and exchange by [[trust-task-context-binding|`taskContext`]] and a task digest), and the registry adds `vetted/1` (a vetter's statement that they checked who you are) and `presented/1`. Every new community accepts exactly these four, fail-closed, until its administrators say otherwise.
- **[[persona-credential|Persona Credentials]]** and **[[invitation-credential|Invitation Credentials]]** — persona linking, and the bootstrap of new participants into communities and rooms.

These credentials form the [[decentralized-trust-graph|Decentralized Trust Graph]] — a graph whose nodes are entities (people, devices, agents, communities, and [[data-rooms|rooms]]) and whose edges are credential pairs. Anyone can trace trust paths between participants. Communities can federate into [[verifiable-trust-network|Verifiable Trust Networks (VTNs)]] — since Fig a running service ([[vtn-service]]) rather than a line in the spec — and [[trust-registries|trust registries]] provide authoritative records of which entities are authorized to perform specific actions within a community — including, now, who may sign commits to which repository. The human root of the whole graph — a community checking that a new member is a real person — is [[peer-identity-vetting]]: existing members check an applicant in person or on video and sign `vetted/1` statements the community counts under a published criterion, and in **hidden** mode the applicant proves in zero knowledge that *k* distinct eligible vetters vouched for them without revealing which.

### Layer 5: The User Experience

[[openvtc|OpenVTC]] is the user-facing TUI that ties it all together for end users. It walks you through binding to your VTA, minting personas and faces, establishing trust relationships with others, and participating in trust communities — including **multiple** trust communities simultaneously, each with its own persona and lifecycle. **0.5.0** (October 2026, Eucalyptus) carried the complete applicant and vetter journeys of peer identity vetting, hidden vetting and a Repos panel for community git namespaces with commit signing through the VTA; **0.9.0** (Fig) adds voting in community elections, data rooms, witnessing through the VTA, did:scid personas, account state kept at the VTA so every device sees it, headless onboarding from the command line, and joining a community by presenting a credential from a government wallet such as swiyu or the EUDI wallet.

The same journeys now exist on a phone. The [[keyring-wallet|Keyring wallet]] (iOS and Android, built on the [[keyring-bifold|Keyring fork of OpenWallet Foundation Bifold]]) began as a peer-to-peer relationship-credential wallet with witnessed exchange and hardware attestation; over September and October 2026 it became a Personal Network Manager over one or several cloud VTAs, speaking Trust Tasks over DIDComm and TSP, able to join a community, approve its agent's requests, and run **the full vetting flow from both sides** — the applicant's and the vetter's desk — just as the TUI does. Through the Fig window it became a place communities *live*: room chat, polls and votes, member badges, wallet-to-wallet messaging through a mailbox per phone, and sign-in to the VTA Farm.

**Communities you live in.** Fig turned the community from something you join into something you take part in. Members propose ideas on a **board**, and each accepted idea becomes an **election** with blind ballots ([[community-governance]]); communities award **badges**, publish badge groups to their trust registry and keep a richer member directory ([[verifiable-trust-community]]); and [[data-rooms|data rooms]] became conversations, with replies, files, live updates and read markers the member's own agent keeps across devices. Above the community, a [[verifiable-trust-network|Verifiable Trust Network]] lists and admits communities and other networks, relays broadcasts one signed hop at a time, and offers a federated directory and a trust-graph view; its operator CLI, `nnm`, completes the set beside the person's `pnm` and the community's `cnm`.

For the **Know Your Developer** case specifically, [[verifiable-git-infrastructure|Verifiable Git Infrastructure (VGI)]] signs git commits with a VTA-held key — the key never leaves the agent, and the commit names its signer by DID in a `Signed-by-DID:` trailer — and lets a project's CI verify, against the community's Trust Registry, that every commit in a pull request was signed by a DID the community currently authorizes. Since Eucalyptus a community can govern the forge namespace itself: [[community-git-namespaces]] bind a VTC to a GitHub or Forgejo organization, with a rights model (namespace admin, repository owner, maintainer, commit signer), separation of duties, break-glass, and a per-community **vgi-bridge** that enforces PR-open gates, required approvals and re-signing on the forge. For **the web**, the [[vta-browser-plugin|VTA Wallet browser plugin]] lets you log into websites as the controller of your VTA DID (passkey ceremony in the browser, keys never leave the VTA), with [[rp-sdk-js]] for the site's server side and [[vti-didcomm-js]] as the browser DIDComm stack; the wallet also carries a management console, a faces manager and a data-rooms pane. For **shared context**, [[data-rooms|data rooms]] give a team, a working group or a family a credential-governed, MLS-encrypted space that its members' agents can also recall from.

On mobile, the [[verifiable-trust-infrastructure|VTI]] also ships two thin native apps — **Authenticator** (a holder's pocket approver for AAL step-up, which now asks the owner before every approval) and **PNM mobile** — both built on a shared Rust UniFFI engine, `vta-mobile-core`.

And for anyone who wants to stand the whole stack up themselves, the [[vti-setup]] repo provides setup guides organized by persona (developer, community manager, sysop), since Fig every one of them re-verified on the release by automated runs — including a Trust Registry in the explore stack and a walkthrough for putting a GitHub organization under a community — and the production path — Kubernetes, Vault, and the Farm — is the [[vtafarm-k8s]] repo. For "which versions go together?", the answer is the latest coordinated release, **Fig** ([[coordinated-releases]]).

## The First Person Network

The ecosystem implements the **First Person Protocol**, described in the [First Person Project white paper](https://firstperson.network/white-paper). The vision is a world where your digital identity belongs to you, your trust relationships are verifiable by anyone, and no single organization can revoke your identity or gatekeep your participation.

The name "first person" is deliberate — this is identity asserted by *you*, not about you by someone else. You create your own DID, you host it on the domain of your choice, and the trust graph is built from mutual attestations between peers.

## Where Things Are Heading

As of 2026-11-28 the ecosystem has shipped **`Fig`** (GA 2026-11-27), its sixth coordinated release and again its largest: 49 days, twenty repositories, 2,218 commits, 328 of them breaking ([[coordinated-releases]]). Its own one-liner is *"one generation, four languages, a network of communities."* Seven weeks earlier, Eucalyptus had been tagged on the day the project was presented to the Linux kernel developers at the Linux Plumbers Conference in Prague ([[kernel-web-of-trust]]). A week before the tag, Verifiable Trust Infrastructure was on the program at Utah's **Park City SEDI Summit** (2026-11-17 → 19), the three-day meeting of state leaders on Utah's **State-Endorsed Digital Identity** model, at which Governor Spencer Cox announced that Colorado, Delaware, Massachusetts, Oklahoma and Wyoming would join Utah in developing it. Glenn Gore (Affinidi), Richard Whitt (GliaNet Alliance), Hart Montgomery (LF Decentralized Trust), Brendan Miller (Harvard Applied Technology Lab, who leads the Keyring wallet) and Drummond Reed (First Person Cooperative) sat on a main-stage panel, *The Future of Digital Trust: SEDI, Verifiable Trust Infrastructure, and Fiduciary AI*, and led a breakout the next day, *Digital Trust in Practice*, billed as a deeper look at what VTI is and how SEDI brings it into practice "through technical demonstrations and interactive discussion" ([agenda](https://www.sedisummit.com/agenda)); Drummond also gave the summit's *Digital Identity 101*. In the weeks before, the [[keyring-wallet|Keyring phone wallet]] had carried a SEDI demo lane — a state-issued guardian credential verified on the phone against a did:webs state root. The frontier has moved on several fronts:

- **One generation, one copy.** Every repository moved to the same dependency generation (affinidi-tdk 0.50, trust-tasks-rs 0.41+), and no lock file anywhere in the release holds two versions of a shared crate. It is unglamorous work, and it is what lets twenty repositories release together.
- **TSP 1.0 in four languages.** The transport the stack had been converging on since Cypress is now a 1.0 protocol in Rust, JavaScript, Go and Dart, with the drafts refused everywhere.
- **Identities beyond did:webvh.** did:webs on KERI v2 and native did:scid are identities a VTA, a community or a member can actually hold — the first time the stack has offered more than one way to be someone.
- **Communities that govern themselves.** Boards, elections, badges, a member directory, and rooms that work as conversations: the community is now somewhere members take part, not only somewhere they are admitted.
- **Networks of communities.** The Verifiable Trust Network went from spec concept to running service in three weeks and joined the release, so communities can federate without sharing rosters.
- **Releases with a support window.** Fig is the first release cut from a green `nightly` onto its own branch, patched there for about ten to twelve weeks; Eucalyptus is still supported beside it, and the next tree, **Ginkgo**, is due around the turn of the year.
- **Hardening as a standing discipline.** Step-up approval verifies the user, queries answer the same whether or not anything matches, and a coordinated advisory update reached ten repositories in two days.

The direction of travel is unchanged but wider: a fully self-contained, publicly deployable trust infrastructure that any community — and now any network of communities — can adopt, with the phone, the browser, the git forge, the AI agent and the shared room as first-class holders of credentials, TSP 1.0 as the common transport, and a formal ToIP specification with a registry underneath.
