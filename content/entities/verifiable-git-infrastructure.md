# Verifiable Git Infrastructure (VGI)

*Repo: [github.com/OpenVTC/verifiable-git-infrastructure](https://github.com/OpenVTC/verifiable-git-infrastructure)*

VGI is the **git layer** of the OpenVTC stack — "the git-layer sibling of [[verifiable-trust-infrastructure]]" — and the most direct realization so far of the **Know Your Developer** problem the whole ecosystem was started to solve (see [[first-person-network]]). It has two halves. The first, from July 2026: a contributor signs git commits with a key held in their [[verifiable-trust-agent|VTA]], and a project's CI asks a community's [[trust-registries|Trust Registry]] whether that DID is authorized to sign for this repository *right now* — **"VGI verifies, the VTC decides."** The second, from late September 2026: a community's [[verifiable-trust-community|VTC]] governs whole GitHub organizations or Forgejo owners — who may create, own, maintain or sign for each repository — and VGI's per-community **bridge** turns those rights into forge roles, repository settings and the required check, reporting back what changes on the forge anyway. **"The VTC decides, the bridge acts, the registry answers."** The rights model itself is described in [[community-git-namespaces]]; this page covers what VGI does with it.

## Why It Exists

Conventional git signing (GPG, SSH keys) binds a commit to a *key*, not to an identity that anyone governs. Onboarding or revoking a contributor means editing allowed-signers files in every repo, and nothing ties the key to a real, accountable person — exactly the gap a sock-puppet or a compromised account slips through. VGI moves the policy out of the repo and into the community: the signing key lives in a VTA (and since October 2026 *stays* there — the VTA signs, the key is never exported), the commit names its signer by DID, and authorization is a live Trust Registry query scoped to the repo. Take a contributor out of the registry and their next commit fails CI everywhere at once.

The forge layer exists for the same reason one level up. A forge's own permission model is a flat list of accounts and roles that someone edits by hand, and nothing connects it to who is actually a member in good standing. With a bridge, the forge roles are a *projection* of rights the community records and governs — grants with a granter, a reason, an expiry, separation of duties and break-glass — and a right a member loses when they leave is taken off the forge by the next projection pass. Nobody edits the forge by hand; what changes there anyway is reported as drift.

It is deliberately *not* a generic git-signing library or a generic forge-management tool: you need a VTA to sign, a registry to verify against, and a VTC to decide.

## Components

Rust workspace (edition 2024, MSRV 1.95), eight crates versioned in lockstep and all published to crates.io. The workspace is at **0.18.2** (2026-10-06), the version `VTI-Eucalyptus` (2026-10-07) points at; it was **0.4.5** at `Cypress`, **0.4.7** at `VTI-Dogwood` and **0.4.12** at `VTI-Eucalyptus-RC-0` ([[coordinated-releases]]) — twenty-nine releases in the nineteen days between, two of them (0.6.0 and 0.8.0) breaking. ### Where the DID claim lives — the Dogwood change

Until Cypress the commit's identity claim was `user.email = <DID#key-id>`: the only place a commit could name its signer, since an sshsig blob carries a raw key and no identity. It worked, but it made every commit unattributable on GitHub and GitLab, which match commits to accounts by email — and it forced people in several communities to keep two settings (`user.email` and the key selector) in step by hand, with a silent wrong-community commit as the failure mode. Dogwood moved the claim into a **`Signed-by-DID:` git trailer** (PR #25, Robert Kwolek, 2026-08-30). The trailer sits inside the commit message, which is part of the signed payload, so it is exactly as tamper-evident as the committer header was; `verify-trust` reads it first and falls back to a DID committer for older commits. A commit carrying *both* a trailer and a DID committer that disagree is a fail-closed verdict, `conflictingSignerDids`. Hook version 2 (0.4.14, #72) writes the trailer into the *final* trailer block, past any `---` line — Dependabot-style messages had been getting the trailer where neither `git log --format='%(trailers)'` nor `verify-trust` reads it, failing `noSignerDid`; `did-git-sign health` reports an `OUTDATED` hook, since upgrading the binary does not replace it. `user.email` and `user.name` are never written.

### The GitHub Action

`.github/actions/verify-trust` (composite) downloads a prebuilt `verify-trust` (Linux x64, macOS arm64/x64, Windows x64 — no Rust toolchain on the runner; `version:` defaults to `latest`, the README example pins `v0.11.0`) and runs it over a commit range. Since #61 it downloads anonymously with `curl` — no `gh`, no token — so it also runs on **Forgejo Actions** runners, referenced by full URL; on GitHub runners the tarball's build-provenance attestation is verified, elsewhere only its SHA-256. For every commit: it must carry a `gpgsig`; it must claim a signer DID (trailer, else DID committer); that DID must resolve — on a *public* host, and not be deactivated — and publish, under `assertionMethod`, the Ed25519 key that signed; the signature must verify; and the registry must hold a grant for the TRQP tuple `(entity = DID, authority = VTC DID, action = git.commit.sign, resource = <forge-host>/owner/repo)`. Inputs of note: `registry-did` (the endpoint is *discovered* from the registry's DID document; `transport: auto|tsp|didcomm|https`, `registry-url` as the explicit HTTPS override), `vtc-did`, `resource` (defaults to the repo, forge-qualified — it alone scopes a signer), `fallback-resource` (the namespace, so namespace-wide grants and admins' implied commit rights count), `resource-format` (`qualified`; `legacy` is refused since 0.8.0), `exempt-keyring` (the committed web-flow keyring, `.github/trusted-platform-keys.asc`), `max-signers`, `resolve-agent-names`, `json`, `verify-attestation`. Every input reaches the script through `env:`, never `${{ }}` in a `run:` body. Verdicts `trusted` / `exempt` pass; `unsigned`, `noSignerDid`, `conflictingSignerDids`, `unresolvedSigner` (including `BlockedHost` and a deactivated DID), `unknownKey`, `badSignature`, `unauthorized`, `registryUnavailable`, `pgpRejected`, `platformSignedEdit`, `platformMergeUnverifiedParent`, `platformMergeAltered` fail — **fails closed at every layer, including registry outage**. The docs insist the check be a *required* status check, and the bridge and `vgi repo init` write the workflow with the DIDs as literals rather than `vars.*`, because any repository admin can change a variable.

## Community git namespaces, from the forge side

A **namespace** binds a VTC to one owner on one forge — `github.com/acme`, `codeberg.org/acme`. Inside it the community records **rights** held by member DIDs on forge-qualified, lowercase resources (normative: `git-ns/right/grant/0.3` in the ToIP Trust Tasks; the model is [[community-git-namespaces]]). VGI's reading of them:

| Right | On | What VGI does with it |
|---|---|---|
| `git.ns.admin` | the namespace | exercised through the VTC and the bridge (bind, adopt, reseat, grants); **no forge role** — making someone an organization owner is left to the community |
| `git.repo.create` | the namespace | the bridge creates the repository and runs the bootstrap plan; the creator becomes owner only when the right is an *explicit* grant (0.3) |
| `git.repo.own` | a repository | projected to the forge's top repository role (`admin` on GitHub by default) |
| `git.repo.maintain` | a repository | projected to `maintain` (GitHub) or the merge allow-list (Forgejo): merge and triage |
| `git.commit.sign` | a repository or the namespace | **no forge role** by default — committers contribute through forks and the required check decides what lands; it is the one right `verify-trust` asks the registry about, so the implied commit right of every `own`, `maintain` and `ns.admin` is published explicitly |

`own` implies `maintain` implies `commit.sign`; the VTC evaluates implication and publishes one TRQP authorization record per live right, under its own authority, naming `impliedBy` but never who granted it or why. The bridge projects only rights held in a person's own name, per *linked* forge account (device flow on GitHub, OAuth2 + PKCE on Forgejo), and each repository's `desiredRoles` carries one role per account — the highest of `own`, `maintain`, `commit.sign` — under the bridge's **role map**, configurable per bridge, forge, namespace and repository and reported back to the VTC (`git-ns/bridge/event` 0.3 `roleMapReported`) so the VTC can re-project when it changes.

What the bridge adds beyond projection:

- **The check that cannot be forged.** Inside an organization with required workflows, the workflow that runs `verify-trust` is held outside the repository and pinned; a pull request cannot satisfy its own check (F3). Where GitHub has no required workflow, the bridge fetches the commits as objects into a throwaway bare repository with a read-only token, runs `verify-trust` as a library with the namespace as fallback resource, and posts "Verify commit trust" as the community's App, with the ruleset pinned to the App's own integration id. The bridge holds a *service grant* of `git.commit.sign` on the namespace, granted by the VTC itself, because it **re-signs Dependabot pull requests** with its own DID on push provenance — only a branch still at the head it read, and only commits GitHub's push delivery attributes to Dependabot.
- **The pull-request gate** (event 0.4 / job 0.5, trust-tasks #723). A forge lets anyone open a pull request on a public repository; a community can say who may (`members`, `committers`, `maintainers`, or named roles). The bridge reports every pull request opened — number, author, actor, draft, from-fork, never title or diff — the VTC decides, and sends a `closePullRequest` job that posts the community's message and closes it. It is hygiene, not the merge gate: it *fails open*, and nothing the required check refuses can be merged through it. A reopen by an owner or maintainer is an override.
- **Required approvals** (0.18.0): `required_approvals = N` puts N approving reviews from accounts with write access — the rights holders the bridge itself projected — into every governed repository's protection, with stale approvals dismissed; a rule asking for fewer is drift.
- **Drift** (reported to the VTC, shown in `openvtc`'s Repos panel, resolved with `git-ns/drift/resolve`): a role added or raised on the forge, a weakened protection, a missing check, disabled Actions. The policy's `role_drift` setting chooses `report` or `enforce`.
- **Manual mode**: a VTC with no bridge still records rights and publishes commit rights to the registry; `vgi repo init` sets each repository up by hand, and roles, drift, the re-sign and the gate do not exist.

The authority chain is deliberately short: the bridge accepts a job only from the one VTC it serves, by issuer and proof before any DID is resolved; the VTC accepts results and events only from the bridge the namespace records; a bridge speaks only for the namespace it serves, and an event naming any resource outside it is refused whole.

### Supply-chain posture of the repo itself

The September hardening (tracked as **SEC-4045**) treats VGI's own CI as part of the trust boundary, because the Action is published for third-party use and the workflows hold crates.io and Release credentials: every `uses:` is pinned to a commit SHA with Dependabot (weekly, 7-day cooldown) moving pins; tokens are `contents: read` by default; `publish.yml` is split into a `package` job (compiles every crate tarball, no OIDC) and a `publish` job (tag-only, `crates-io` environment, `--no-verify`, so no dependency build script runs beside the registry credential) — resumable, publishing the forge crates and the bridge from the tag; release tarballs (now `verify-trust` on four targets and `vgi-bridge` on three) carry build-provenance attestations; a `CODEOWNERS` routes `.github/`, `policy.rs`, `verify-trust/` and the manifests to `@OpenVTC/openvtc-admins`; CI scans dependencies for advisories (#107) and builds the bridge container (#108). Two regression gates guard the shape: `action-selftest.yml` runs the Action with shell syntax in its inputs on every PR and fails if any of it executes, and a structural test parses every workflow and asserts no `${{ }}` inside a `run:` scalar and no unpinned `uses:`. The SEC-4045 findings numbered VGI-01 through VGI-08 are each closed with a regression test: VGI-01 (the platform-merge check ran in the caller's working directory — now from the fetch, and an empty range is refused), VGI-02 (any Ed25519 key in a DID document could sign — now `assertionMethod` only), VGI-04 (a deactivated did:webvh kept verifying — now `unresolvedSigner`), VGI-08 (`gpgsig-sha256` headers left in the payload).

## Relationship to OpenVTC

`did-git-sign` was born inside [[openvtc]] — auto-configured by its setup wizard since v0.1.6, per-repo persona selection under multi-community (T8) — and the verify side landed there first too (openvtc #152 verify-trust, #154 PGP exemption keyring + Action packaging, #155 org-fallback grants + committed web-flow keyring, July 2026). VGI was then extracted with history (first commit 2026-07-18) and openvtc #159 dropped the vendored crate to consume the published one (0.4.5 at Cypress), pinning the Action for its own dogfooding CI (#160). A standing obligation is recorded in both repos: VGI's `vta-sdk` line must track OpenVTC's, or two `vta-sdk` copies land in the OpenVTC binary — and the obligation is discharged only by a *published* release, not by main. In the Eucalyptus window it bit on every vta-sdk minor from 0.43 to 0.63, which is why releases 0.4.13, 0.5.2, 0.6.0, 0.8.1, 0.9.0, 0.10.0 and 0.11.0 are each one commit long, and why OpenVTC's own dependency PRs name the did-git-sign version they waited for.

The relationship inverted in October. OpenVTC 0.5.0's Repos panel (#425, #475) **provisions did-git-sign itself**: it mints a `did:key`, grants it — through OpenVTC's admin session — `admin` of the persona's own sub-context narrowed to `sign-sshsig` (or `key-export` for an older VTA), proves the grant by asking the VTA for a real SSHSIG signature over a vgi-core-encoded message, stores the identity as a did-git-sign **named profile** with the library's own calls, and runs `did-git-sign enable --profile` in each checkout — no `init`, no `pnm` grant. Three VGI releases were cut for that path in two days: 0.15.1 (`init` finds personas in sub-contexts), 0.18.1 (`auto` falls back when a `key-export`-only credential is refused `permissionDenied`, so OpenVTC-provisioned identities kept signing when the VTA was upgraded) and 0.18.2 (did-git-sign reads the same OS store OpenVTC writes to, so a Linux signer finds its credential). OpenVTC also consumes `vgi-core` directly (0.18.1) and ships the member-facing half of git namespaces — the Repos panel over `git-ns/view`, grants, drift resolve, break-glass — that the bridge's projection is the other end of.
